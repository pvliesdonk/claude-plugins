# Interviews, Themes and Free Text

Qualitative material resists the move that makes other representations easy: you cannot size it. The
deeper difference is that here **the display is not a report of the analysis — it is where the
analysis happens.** Condensation, display and conclusion-drawing run concurrently, with an
uncomfortable corollary: *you know what you display*, and what never entered a cell never entered the
argument. So draw the display early, on partial data; its first job is to show what you have not
collected. (Theory with thirty years' standing, from Miles and Huberman — no controlled trial shows
analysts working from matrices reach better conclusions than analysts working in prose.)

## The recipe

1. **Write the decision rules before the first cell, and publish them beside the display.** Five,
   minimum: what defines a **row** (case, participant, event, document — if two rows are not the same
   kind of thing, comparison down the column is void); what defines a **column**, and whether it came
   from the interview guide, the coding frame or an emerging construct; **what may enter a cell** —
   verbatim, paraphrase, rating or presence mark, one register per column, since mixed registers are
   how a matrix quietly starts implying measurement; **who entered it, from what**; and **what a
   blank means** — absent, not asked, not coded, declined are four facts that look identical, so give
   them distinct visible states. If you cannot say what a blank means, you are not ready to draw.
   Name the ordering principle in the title (time-ordered, role-ordered, explanatory): the
   arrangement is itself a claim. If a column keeps collapsing into its neighbour, the format is a
   hypothesis about the material and it has failed.
2. **Say whether the display is a thinking instrument or evidence.** Displays are strong for thinking
   in any tradition and much weaker as evidence in constructionist, narrative and discursive work,
   where the warrant lives in the extended extract with its sequence intact. That objection — that
   condensation into cells destroys the phenomenon — is serious and unresolved; preserve it rather
   than picking a side. And a display is a *marked* act in qualitative reporting: it reads as a claim
   that the same frame was applied to every case, so if the frame was improvised for some, do not
   draw it.
3. **Put each theme in a sentence with a verb before drawing anything.** A theme's warrant is not
   frequency — keyness is not a quantifiable measure but whether it answers the research question, so
   a pattern mentioned once by the only person positioned to notice it can outrank one mentioned by
   everybody (which may be the interview guide talking). Themes do not *emerge*; they are
   constructed, so a layout that looks found rather than argued lies about where the work happened.
   If the only available sentence is "participants talked about training", you have a **domain
   summary**, not a theme.
4. **Never size a theme by extract count.** A thematic map may encode relations you argue for —
   subsumes, is in tension with, precedes, conditions — and never counts or magnitude: no node sized
   by mentions, no line weight for frequency, no left-to-right order for importance, no overlap for
   co-occurrence. Test: **if a reader could read your map as a Venn diagram or a bar chart, redraw
   it.** Ambiguous geometry does not stay ambiguous; readers resolve it into the quantitative grammar
   they already know. Label every link, or the reader supplies a relation you never claimed.
5. **Treat counting as genuinely contested, not forbidden.** The prohibition is a reflexive-TA
   commitment following from a particular view of what a theme is, not a universal rule — codebook
   TA, framework analysis and content analysis count as standard practice. The counter-case is
   equally serious: numbers are already pervasive ("some", "most", "recurrent" are quantity claims),
   so the real error is *concealed* counting, and simple quasi-statistics guard an analyst against
   cherry-picking their own favourite extracts. Naming your tradition settles more of this than any
   argument about numbers. **Count only when all three hold:** **internal** to this corpus, claiming
   nothing about a population; a **fixed opportunity set**, so everyone counted was asked and silence
   is interpretable; and it **disciplines you** rather than persuading the reader — the moment its
   job is to make a stakeholder confident, it has changed sides. Report raw numerator and denominator
   with the opportunity set named ("mentioned by 7 of the 14 asked about handover"), never as a
   percentage, never on a comparable axis, never fused to a theme's name.
6. **For free text, name the job: invitation, gist or evidence.** Only the first two are open to
   bag-of-words forms. The case against the word cloud is usually made badly — yes, font size
   dominates recall and carries a position effect, and size judgments are biased by word length and
   letter shape, but that study found the bias *smaller* than folklore claims, so overstating it
   loses the argument. The stronger objection is not perceptual and no redesign fixes it: a cloud
   destroys **syntax**; **negation** ("not safe" becomes *safe*, inverting findings); **collocation**
   ("customer service" splits into two apparent concerns); **speaker**, so one voluble respondent is
   indistinguishable from thirty brief ones; and it hides **the stoplist and stemmer**, undisclosed
   analytic decisions that change the output. Never use a cloud to support an inference; as an
   invitation it is legitimate, declared as one. Prefer concordance lines — words in context is the
   default, not the advanced option — or a ranked frequency list with its stoplist stated. Offer the
   replacement before attacking the cloud.
7. **Never present a topic-model topic as a theme.** A topic is a weighted list of co-occurring
   terms; a theme is an argued construction, and the gap is a human reading that must be visible.
   Optimising the standard selection statistic can make topics *less* interpretable — held-out
   likelihood is often negatively correlated with human judgments of interpretability. If a topic
   appears at all, it carries exemplar documents, the value of *k* with a note that *k* was chosen
   rather than found, and the fact that another seed yields different topics.
8. **Attach the selection story to every quote.** An extract illustrates a claim the analysis
   established; it is rarely the evidence for one. Each carries an **ID** (so a reader can see that
   six of eight quotes came from two people — tally your speakers before finalising), a **selection
   story** (most articulate instance of a pattern coded in N sources / a deviant case / the only
   counter-instance / chosen for clarity), and a **typical-or-extreme marker**, stated not implied.
   For every major claim show **one disconfirming case at equal prominence** — same type, same
   position, not a footnote. The chain claim → code → extract → source must be walkable both ways and
   must exist before anyone asks; provenance reconstructed later is provenance invented. Personas and
   journey maps fuse evidence with invention, so mark each element evidenced or inferred *inside* the
   artefact — a caption is not in the screenshot — with a provenance strip and an expiry date. And
   "member checked" is a badge unless you say what was returned, to whom, and what changed.
9. **Pair strands; do not convert them.** A joint display earns its name only through an explicit
   **integration column** on every row: **confirmation** (with what each strand independently
   supports, so the agreement is not circular), **expansion** (in which direction), or
   **discordance** (and what the disagreement *means*). Discordance is the row people delete and the
   one worth most: high satisfaction beside interviews full of workarounds is not a data problem to
   reconcile but a finding about what the item measured. Keep both strands in native units and
   comparable space, and build it during analysis — a display assembled at write-up can only agree
   with conclusions already written around.
10. **Meet "but how many?" with one of five moves, none of them capitulation.** The asker is not
    innumerate; they have a decision and one heuristic for sizing risk. (a) **Answer the real
    question** — "how confident should I be acting on this?" — with the structure of the evidence:
    independent sources, consistency, the disconfirming case, fit with known mechanisms. (b) **Give
    the honest count** where the three conditions hold; withholding one you legitimately have looks
    evasive. (c) **Pair, don't convert** — statistic beside the theme that explains it, verdict
    between them. (d) **Substitute severity for frequency**: "three of fourteen hit this, and all
    three lost data". (e) **Concede and propose the instrument** — sometimes prevalence really is the
    decision-relevant quantity, and the qualitative work produced the right item to ask.

## Required moves

Competent analysts write good prose and skip these. Check each:

- [ ] **Decision rules written first and published beside the display** — especially what a blank means.
- [ ] **Thinking instrument or evidence**, declared in the caption.
- [ ] **Every theme a sentence with a verb** someone could disagree with.
- [ ] **Geometry audited for smuggled quantity** — size, weight, order, overlap, distance each meaningless-by-design or explained.
- [ ] **Tradition named**; any count passing all three conditions, with numerator, denominator and opportunity set.
- [ ] **Stoplist, stemming and blank rate disclosed** on any text display; contribution distribution checked before aggregating at all.
- [ ] **Selection story and ID on every extract**, plus one disconfirming case at equal prominence.
- [ ] **Integration verdict on every joint-display row**, discordance rows written in full.
- [ ] **The "how many" answer rehearsed beforehand**, so the move is not chosen under pressure.

## Failure modes

- **The write-up display** — built last from conclusions already fixed in prose; the tell is no empty cells and no surprises.
- **The undifferentiated blank** — a reader infers disagreement from a gap in the fieldwork; and its cousin, rigour by appearance, where a clean grid implies an exhaustive corpus that was opportunistic.
- **The interview guide with arrows** — domain summaries drawn as structure, recapitulating the question list and calling it a finding.
- **The count-sized map** — bigger bubble, stronger theme: the most legible thing on the page and the least defensible.
- **The percentage that escapes** — a disciplining count lifted onto a slide, then into a chart, where it reads as prevalence.
- **The cloud as executive summary of open text** — tokenisation artefacts presented as findings at the moment of decision.
- **The labelled topic** — an analyst's interpretation sitting where model output appears to be.
- **The pull-quote slide** — one uncited quote doing the work of proof, often from the most fluent participant: the qualitative equivalent of a truncated axis.
- **The decorative joint display** — two columns side by side with nothing said about their relationship. Adjacency is not integration.
- **Capitulation** — the demanded percentage chart with a caveat in small type. The chart travels, the caveat does not, and once a finding is rendered in quantitative grammar the grammar wins.
- **Refusal** — principled, and the decision then gets made on whatever number someone else supplies.
