# The Monitoring Dashboard

Defined by a reader who is **not asking a question**. Dwell is three to fifteen seconds but it
repeats — daily, hourly, on the way past a screen — so the learning cost is paid once and amortised
over hundreds of glances, which is what licenses the highest density of any occasion. The author is
never present; control of the view may exist but should not be needed; and the next action is not to
decide or learn but to **notice** — that nothing requires them, or that something does. This
occasion punishes the tile with no normal band: a number with no notion of what a bad number looks
like hands back the judgment the surface was built to supply. Teams build these competently and skip
the four questions that decide whether one should exist at all.

**"Dashboard" names a family, not a form.** The strict at-a-glance monitoring definition is
practitioner convention (Few), a broader definition admits narrative (Wexler et al.), and survey
evidence of real dashboards finds most of them serving awareness, learning and general
communication rather than monitoring — many of them static. Applying at-a-glance rules to a
communication surface is a category error; name which kind you are building first.

## The recipe

1. **Sort the request out loud** — dashboard ("is anything wrong?", no question asked), report (a
   known recurring question on a schedule), or analysis (a new question, once). Most things called
   dashboards are reports with a filter bar; that is fine, but the rules differ.
2. **Run the four-question gate.** Name **the person** (not a role — "the leadership team" means
   nobody), **the cadence**, **the normal band per metric**, and **the action on breach and who has
   authority to take it**. Any gap and you write an answer or a report instead. Convention, but
   derived from the occasion's variables rather than asserted.
3. **Give every tile a threshold, or delete the tile.** Not shrink it, not move it to a second tab.
   Ask per tile: what action does a red state cause, and who takes it?
4. **Design for the hundredth glance, not the first.** Learning cost is paid once; comprehension
   speed is paid every day.
5. **Keep the layout stable.** Rearranging destroys the spatial habit that makes a three-second
   glance possible.
6. **Revisit whether anyone still looks.** An unwatched dashboard is a liability — it looks like
   coverage while providing none.

## Required moves

- [ ] **Request sorted** — dashboard, report or analysis, said before designing.
- [ ] **All four gate questions answered**, person and action on breach included.
- [ ] **Every tile thresholded**, or removed.
- [ ] **Status not carried by hue alone** — pair colour with position, an icon and a written state.
      Red/amber/green alone is this occasion's characteristic accessibility failure.
- [ ] **Layout stable** across releases.
- [ ] **Usage checked** after launch, not assumed.

## Failure modes

- **The wish-list dashboard** — every requested metric, no thresholds, no owner, no consequence.
  The standard failure, and its causes are organisational, not graphical: no chart work fixes it.
- **The report with a filter bar**, judged by dashboard rules it never tried to meet.
- **The analysis tool called a dashboard**, assessed for glanceability it cannot have.
- **Thresholdless tiles** — values with no signal.
- **The dashboard delivered instead of an answer** — see `executive-briefing.md`; the gate catches it
  at question four, because there was never a breach to define, only a claim to dodge.
