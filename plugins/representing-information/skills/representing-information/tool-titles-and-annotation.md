# Titles and Annotation

The title above a chart is the most tested, most consistently replicated, highest-leverage channel in
the artefact — and the one no review process polices, because reviewers scrutinise the encoding and
read the title as a label. Annotation is the reduction that *adds*: it removes search, not data.

## The move
- **Write the title as the finding, not the topic.** "Renewals fell a fifth after the price change",
  never "Renewals by quarter". If you cannot write the sentence, you do not yet have a finding.
- **Annotate more than feels comfortable.** Stokes et al. had 302 participants rank line charts from
  no text through heavy annotation to text-only: **the most heavily annotated charts were preferred**
  — over sparser ones and over text alone. The instinct that text on a chart is clutter is wrong.
- **Annotate at the semantic level you want the takeaway at.** Text about statistics and relations
  produced statistical and relational takeaways. Placement is a decision too: the claim goes in the
  title, the thing explaining one mark goes beside that mark.
- **Mute rather than delete.** Everything the claim depends on stays visible at reduced weight —
  Tufte's layering, which is how a dense display still has one dominant message.

## The evidence, and why it should worry you
Kong, Liu & Karahalios (CHI 2018): viewers saw identical charts under differently slanted titles,
derived **opposing** main messages, recalled the title's slant — and **a majority still rated the
visualisation unbiased**. The 2019 follow-up pushed to outright contradictory titles: more bias was
detected, but the majority *still* perceived the chart as impartial, and **recall aligned with the
title more often than with the chart**. The title is simultaneously the easiest place to lie and the
place a lie is least likely to be caught.

The counterweight: O'Brien & Lauer (n=256) found accurate prose placed *beside* a deceptively encoded
chart did **not** rescue interpretation. Text is powerful *with* the encoding's suggestion and weak
*against* it. So if the chart cannot support the title, change the title, not the caption — and fix
a distorted encoding at the encoding.

Ajani et al.'s "focus" bundle — assertion headline, one highlight colour, connected annotation —
improved **memory for the highlighted pattern**. Nothing in that design could measure whether the
highlighted pattern was the right one. **Treat the bundle as a demonstrated persuasion technology**
and aim it only at findings you would defend under cross-examination.

## "One message per chart", handled carefully
Right for **one-pass explanatory artefacts** — a slide, a report figure, a press graphic.
Implemented by **layering, not deletion**: enforce "one *dominant* visual message", not "delete the
context". **False as a universal law** — exploratory displays, operational dashboards and reference
material legitimately carry many messages, because the reader is interrogating, not receiving. No
experiment supports the literal rule; its only empirical backing is the focus finding, which is about
emphasis, not exclusivity.

## Failure modes
- **The wasted title** — a topic label over a chart with a real finding; the strongest channel spent restating the axis names.
- **The overshooting headline** — an assertion the data supports directionally but not in magnitude; invisible to readers, so the author must catch it.
- **Caveat-by-caption** — believing accurate surrounding prose neutralises a misleading picture.
- **Splitting a multivariate finding into six single-message charts** so the interaction — the actual finding — disappears. The one-message rule's most expensive failure.
- **Framing by omission** — highlighting the favourable subset and leaving the rest unremarked.
- **Citing Ajani et al. for the one-message rule** — they tested emphasis, not exclusivity.
