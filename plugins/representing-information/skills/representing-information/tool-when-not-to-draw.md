# When Not to Draw, and How to Critique

Nobody was ever praised for the chart they did not make. "What chart should this be?" arrives already
answered in one respect — that it should be a chart. It should not always be one. And when one
exists, the order you review it in decides whether the review is worth running.

## The move — cognitive fit
**Cognitive fit is the one real theory here**, and unusually strong: it reconciled a contradictory
1980s literature by making the *task* the variable rather than the representation (Vessey).

- **Tables are symbolic** — they suit extracting discrete values: lookup, exact comparison, threshold checks.
- **Graphs are spatial** — they suit perceiving relationships: trends, associations, shape, exceptions.

Mismatch costs both time and accuracy. Where both tasks are genuinely present, give **both forms** —
a chart for the shape, a table beneath for the values. The chart with a label on every mark is the
artefact produced by refusing to decide, and it serves both tasks worse than either dedicated form.

## The genuine pro-table arguments
Worth holding, and best made by Gelman — who co-authored the pro-graph demonstration and then wrote
the adversarial case against his own position:

- **Exactness** — a table gives the number; a graph gives a position to estimate from, with error.
- **Checkability** — a table can be audited and recomputed by someone who disbelieves you.
- **No imposed smoothing** — every graph embeds the author's binning, scaling and framing choices.
- **Survival under decontextualisation** — a table pasted into an email still means what it meant.

"Graphs beat tables" is a live disagreement, not a settled question; knowing only one side argues badly.

## Apply it — the ladder
Take the first form that carries the finding.

| Form | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Sentence** | The finding is one comparison — "Churn rose from 4.1% to 6.8% between Q1 and Q3." |
| **Number** | There is one quantity the reader needs the value of, with its unit and denominator |
| **Table** | n is small, values will be quoted, units are heterogeneous, or the artefact must be auditable |
| **Chart** | The shape, outlier or trajectory **is** the finding |

Below roughly eight rows by three columns a table usually wins — a practitioner rule of thumb, not a
measured boundary, and dominated by the task question anyway. The controlling test: **a picture is
warranted when the claim is about a relationship the eye can verify.** If the caption has to tell the
reader the relationship, the picture is decoration on a sentence. Handing back the sentence is the
finding delivered, not a smaller deliverable.

## Then: the three critique gates, in order
The order is the whole method, and the argument for it is analytic rather than empirical: **every
item on a craft checklist sits at the encoding level**, so a checklist is structurally incapable of
failing a chart for answering the wrong question. Score a beautifully formatted wrong answer and you
get a high score.

1. **Task.** An **unbriefed** critic states, in their own words, what question it answers and what
   decision that feeds. Compare against the pre-brief's Question line. Divergence is an
   **abstraction** failure — and you stop. Encoding feedback here is not merely premature; it is
   harmful, because it creates the impression the chart is being improved.
2. **Takeaway.** Show it for about five seconds, remove it, ask what it said — or give a naive reader
   as long as they like and ask for the finding in one sentence. Compare against the Claim line.
   Divergence is an **encoding** failure, and craft feedback now becomes useful. Practitioner
   doctrine, no experimental base; five seconds is a convention, not a threshold.
3. **Checklist.** Scored, repeatable craft review (Evergreen & Emery's ~24 items). Its real value is
   catching **regression** across edits or across a team. Item selection is professional judgment and
   the 90–100% band is convention, not a validated cut-off.

**Running gate 3 first is the classic waste of a critique** — and the default, because it is the only
gate that produces a number.

## Failure modes
- **Showing the analysis rather than the answer** — a chart carrying one comparison a sentence would state faster and more precisely.
- **The three-row bar chart** — a table with decoration.
- **Charting heterogeneous units** — a shared scale where the comparison is meaningless by construction.
- **Briefing the gate-1 critic** — telling them the question before asking what question it answers. Gate 1 cannot be run twice on the same person.
- **Treating the checklist score as the review** — a number is not an argument, and this one is scoped to a single level.
- **Time-and-errors as general evidence** — read speed says nothing about whether the right thing was shown.
