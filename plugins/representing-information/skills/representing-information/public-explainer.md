# The Public Explainer

The only occasion where the reader has no obligation to be there. Dwell is unbounded in principle
and near zero in practice: attention is **voluntary and abandonable**, every scroll is re-earned,
and a reader who is not gripped simply leaves at no cost and with no signal to you. The author is
absent, this reader has no domain context to fall back on and nobody to ask, and control of the view
is technically ample and behaviourally almost unused. The next action is to keep reading, or to
leave. That inverts the assumption every other occasion rests on — elsewhere you may assume the
reader wants the information; here you may not. It punishes design that assumes engagement rather
than earning it, and it fails silently. Capable teams build beautiful graphics and skip the
**annotation** that makes the finding survive a screenshot.

**The cost ordering** (practitioner telemetry, not experiment): scrolling is free, clicking is
expensive, hovering is invisible — no affordance on touch, discovered by accident on desktop. Treat
hover-revealed content as decoration however useful it is.

## The recipe

1. **Write the finding as a sentence and put it inside the picture**, near the marks it refers to.
   Not in the standfirst, not in the paragraph below. The graphic will be screenshotted and shared
   without its article, so self-containment is a distribution requirement as well as a comprehension
   one.
2. **Design the no-interaction path first.** A reader who scrolls past and touches nothing must get
   the point.
3. **Assume no domain context.** Define the unit, the population, the time span and which direction
   is "good", inside the graphic.
4. **Annotate specific marks** — the moment, the outlier, the crossing — not the chart in general.
5. **Never hide load-bearing content behind a click or a hover.** This is the one rule with no
   dissent on either side of the field's interactivity debate.
6. **Justify any interaction by its function** — verification, access to the full dataset, letting
   the reader vary your assumptions — not by its existence. "Explore the data" is not a function.

**Keep the evidence straight when someone quotes it at you.** The famous "85% of readers ignore
interactive graphics" is a mangling: what was measured is that about 85% did not click one prominent
button. That licenses "do not hide the point behind a button", not "readers ignore graphics". And
15% of a large readership is not nobody — a second, additive layer of interaction can be a
defensible choice, as long as the other 85% still get the finding.

## Required moves

- [ ] **Finding written out inside the graphic**, not in the caption or the prose.
- [ ] **Static-and-annotated first**, interaction only as an additive second layer.
- [ ] **No domain knowledge assumed** — unit, denominator, span, direction defined.
- [ ] **Nothing load-bearing behind a click or hover.**
- [ ] **Annotation pointed at specific marks.**
- [ ] **Interactive budget stated honestly** — expensive to build, reaching a minority.

## Failure modes

- **"Explore the data yourself"** — high build cost, near-zero engagement, and the one thing the
  reader needed three clicks deep. The characteristic failure here.
- **The finding in the caption** — shared as an image, the graphic arrives meaning nothing.
- **Hover-only labels**, usually where the actual numbers were put.
- **Assuming engagement** on the one occasion where the reader demonstrably is not committed.
- **Assuming domain context** — unexplained jargon, an unlabelled index, a rate with no denominator.
  A paper's reader could look these up; this one will not.
