# The Poster

The only occasion with **two dwell times and two readers on one sheet**. At roughly two metres,
someone walking past gives it three to ten seconds and makes a physical decision: stop, or keep
walking. At half a metre, the person who stopped gives it a minute or two and decides whether this
is worth a conversation. Control of the view is nil — no zoom, no re-flow, and a printed sheet
cannot adapt to the light in the hall. Author presence is intermittent: the poster is on the wall
for hours whether you are beside it or not. So its first job is to **stop someone walking**, and
nothing else in the design matters until that happens. This occasion punishes the shrunken paper.
Capable researchers produce accurate, well-referenced content and skip the two-metre layer
entirely — everything sized for the close reader who therefore never arrives.

## The recipe

1. **Write the finding as one sentence and set it in the largest type on the sheet**, before laying
   anything else out. "Freight, not price, explains the margin gap" is a headline; "An Analysis of
   Margin Drivers, 2019–2024" is a filing label in large type.
2. **Assign every element to a distance** — two metres or half a metre, nothing in between. Anything
   sized for neither serves nobody.
3. **Keep the second layer thin enough to read standing up**: the evidence for the claim, the method
   in outline, n and uncertainty, and the honest limitation. Nowhere near paper-figure density, and
   not a report figure pasted across.
4. **Put depth elsewhere** — a handout, a preprint, a scannable link. The poster is not the paper.
5. **Test at two metres, literally.** Print it, back away, and see whether a stranger can state the
   claim in three seconds.
6. **Assume bad light and print colour shift.** Rely on what survives: luminance contrast, direct
   labels, position. No load-bearing distinction on hue alone; generous leading; nothing at the
   bottom edge a reader must crouch for.

**What the evidence actually says.** The billboard layout (#BetterPoster) went viral on an argument
and has since been tested, with real but mixed results: complete-sentence assertion headings beat
topical ones on recall; a randomised trial (n=94) found **no difference on general topic knowledge**
but significantly better scores on the two key learning points; and an assessment of 103 posters
found the format ahead on first impression while **over-simplified versions did worse than
well-made traditional posters**. The defensible summary: the assertion headline is the transferable
finding, the layout is a good default rather than a guarantee, and stripping it further is penalised.

## Required moves

- [ ] **The finding, as a claim, in the largest type.**
- [ ] **Every element assigned to a viewing distance.**
- [ ] **Second layer readable standing up** — evidence, method, n, uncertainty, limitation.
- [ ] **Two-metre test run with a real stranger.**
- [ ] **Depth offloaded** to a handout or link.
- [ ] **Designed to work unattended**, because for most of its life it is.

## Failure modes

- **The paper, shrunk** — tiny axis labels, an abstract nobody reads, a topic title, page layout.
- **The topic title in large type** — large, but nothing to stop for.
- **Nothing readable at two metres** — the close reader never arrives.
- **The over-simplified billboard** — a huge sentence with nothing behind it, which the evidence
  shows underperforms a well-made traditional poster.
- **Colour-coded categories with no labels**, unreadable under hall lighting and print variation.
