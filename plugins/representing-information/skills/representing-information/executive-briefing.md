# The Executive Briefing

Thirty seconds to two minutes per page, and the attention inside those seconds is adversarial in a
good way: the reader is hunting for the number that undercuts you. The next action is to **decide**.
Control of the view is nil — a printed page or a PDF. And the variable people get wrong is author
presence: a pre-read pack is author-absent even when you also present it, because the opinion that
matters formed in a car on Tuesday evening while you were not there. So the claim must be complete
*and* instant; nothing else asks for both at once. This occasion punishes the un-asserted page — one
that shows correct numbers and concludes nothing. Capable analysts produce accurate, well-built
pages and skip the **assertion headline**, the **comparator**, and the discipline of sequencing the
follow-up instead of cramming it in.

**Say plainly what this is.** Almost the entire apparatus below — answer-first structure, action
titles, the titles test, the comparator-bearing tile — is consulting convention with a plausible
rationale and **no controlled evidence** behind it. No study shows action titles improve board
decisions. Teach it, use it, and do not call it a finding; anyone who says the pyramid is "proven"
is laundering doctrine into evidence.

## The recipe

1. **Write the headline first, as a conclusion.** "Margin recovery is two quarters behind plan
   because of freight, not price" is a headline; "Q3 Margin Analysis" is a filing label. If you
   cannot write it, the page is premature however good the charts are.
2. **Run the titles test** on the assembled pack: read only the titles, in order. If they do not
   reproduce the argument, the pack has material and no argument. Executives read this way whether
   or not you designed for it.
3. **Give every number a comparator** — plan, prior period, threshold, peer — and the direction.
   "Churn 4.2%" is a measurement; "4.2% against a 3.1% plan, worsening for three quarters" is a
   finding. Choosing which comparator is your job, not the reader's.
4. **One page, one decision-relevant assertion**, plus the evidence that would change your mind —
   not the evidence that supports you.
5. **Pre-build the follow-up as the next page.** Density is not the answer to anticipated questions;
   sequence is.
6. **Assume the author is absent and the page is photocopied.** Anything load-bearing that lives in
   speaker notes is not in the pack, and anything riding on colour alone will not survive greyscale.

## Required moves

- [ ] **Assertion headline** on every page.
- [ ] **Titles test run** before anyone else sees the pack.
- [ ] **Comparator and direction** on every number.
- [ ] **The disconfirming evidence** included, not just the supporting evidence.
- [ ] **Follow-up sequenced behind**, current page kept thin.
- [ ] **Designed for the pre-read**, not for the meeting.

## Failure modes

- **The dashboard instead of the answer** — the defining failure, and defensive rather than
  analytic: a dashboard cannot be wrong because it asserts nothing. If the request was a decision,
  run the four-question gate in `monitoring-dashboard.md`; it will fail at "action on breach".
- **Topic titles** over four competent charts, recommendation in the speaker notes — so the board
  invents its own conclusion, usually the wrong one.
- **The naked number** — large, uncompared, and read as significant because it is large.
- **The everything page** — anticipated questions crammed around the assertion until it is noise.
- **Burying the conclusion for suspense** — this is a decision document, not a narrative, and it
  costs you the reader's most attentive thirty seconds.
