# The Live Talk Slide

A projected chart gets five to forty seconds of dwell; the reader cannot zoom, hover, re-read or
print, and cannot decide when the next slide arrives. Against that, **you are standing there**, and
the reader's next action is not to decide or verify but to follow an argument. So the slide is not
the message — narration plus slide is the message, and this is the occasion that licenses the most
aggressive reduction available anywhere. It punishes anything that asks to be read silently: the
moment the audience starts reading, the half of the artefact carrying the argument is lost. Capable
presenters draw the chart competently and often write a decent headline; what they skip is
**signalling** — nothing foregrounded — and they under-use the licence to delete.

## The recipe

1. **Write the headline as a sentence with a verb.** Assertion above, visual evidence beneath. This
   structure has several controlled studies behind it, but the effects are modest and concentrated
   in complex material and delayed recall — a well-supported convention, not settled law. If you
   cannot write the sentence, you do not yet have the point.
2. **Signal one element.** Foreground what you will say; mute everything else into a receding
   neutral. Highest leverage, lowest cost — if there is time for exactly one edit, make it this.
3. **Delete rather than shrink.** Spare series, second axes, decorative furniture. Removal, not
   reduction; sunk cost is not an argument for keeping something on screen.
4. **Label directly, in place — labels yes, sentences no.** Short labels next to the marks help
   (the redundancy principle was revised on exactly this point); a paragraph of the words you are
   about to speak competes with you.
5. **Build complex material in stages** so the audience decodes with you rather than ahead of you.
6. **Decide where the permanent record lives** before you present, and let it be a memo or handout —
   the slide is underspecified on purpose, and archived it becomes an artefact it was never built to
   be.

("One idea per slide" is craft convention, never tested — but it is what steps 1, 2 and 5 produce by
construction, which is a better defence than a citation that does not exist.)

## Required moves

- [ ] **Assertion headline** — a claim with a verb, not "Q3 Revenue by Region".
- [ ] **One element signalled**, everything else muted. The move most reliably skipped.
- [ ] **Deleted, not shrunk** — nothing on screen you will not mention.
- [ ] **Direct labels, no legend across the slide**, and no spoken sentences duplicated on it.
- [ ] **Staged reveal** for anything complex.
- [ ] **Back-row legibility checked** as the room will see it — smallest label sized for the back
      row, with projector colour shift and crushed darks assumed.
- [ ] **Record located elsewhere** than the deck.

## Failure modes

- **The pasted report figure** — four panels and a caption, silently asking for sixty seconds of
  reading you are not going to give. It fails coherence and signalling at once. See `tool-recutting.md`.
- **The slide that says what you are saying** — the audience reads and stops listening.
- **The topic title** — names a subject, asserts nothing, leaves the point to be guessed.
- **Nothing highlighted** — a correct chart the audience is still searching when you have moved on.
- **The deck as archive** — author-present design shipped to people who read it without you.
