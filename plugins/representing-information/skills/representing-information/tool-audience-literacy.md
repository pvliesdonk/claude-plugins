# Audience and Literacy

Designers calibrate on the room they are standing in, and the room is not the audience. Two
correctives: the general-population floor is lower than most authors assume, and the expert ceiling
is lower too.

## The move — set the floor from evidence
- **Roughly one in three of a general population reads graphs poorly.** Galesic &
  García-Retamero's 13-item scale, on nationally representative German (n=495) and US (n=492)
  samples, put about a third of each in the low band. That is not a tail.
- **Literacy and numeracy dissociate.** They correlate only moderately, so screening for one tells
  you little about the other. Norms do not travel either — a Japanese replication hit ceiling on the
  same scales, so "one in three" is a finding about particular populations.
- **Education is not a guarantee.** Lipkus et al. found a substantial minority of *highly educated*
  samples could not convert between percentages and proportions, or say which of 1%, 5% and 10% was
  the largest risk.
- **Low-literacy readers scan differently, not slower.** Eye-tracking (Okan et al.) shows they
  allocate proportionally less fixation to the meaning-bearing regions — axis, legend, scale — and
  answer from a surface impression. The missing information is *unvisited*, so improving a legend
  nobody looks at buys nothing. **Move the load-bearing fact into the title, as a sentence.**
- **The icon-array result is an interaction, not a main effect.** Visual aids helped most those with
  **low numeracy but adequate graph literacy**; readers low on *both* gained little and could be
  harmed. An icon array is a translation device, not a literacy fix. Animating pictographs added
  nothing (randomised, n=187).
- **A literacy score is a coarse signal, not a dial.** Brockbank et al. (N=1,113) ran two standard
  instruments: scores correlate overall while error patterns diverge, and neither test's design
  categories predicted behaviour. Use literacy to set what the default must survive; never build
  graded versions keyed to a measured score.

## Apply it — the expert end
- **"My audience are statisticians" is not a licence.** Belia et al. surveyed authors published in
  *Psychological Science*, *AJPH* and *MSSE*: most **could not position error bars to indicate
  p=.05**, and many did not distinguish confidence intervals from standard-error bars at all. The
  professional audience for error bars cannot read error bars.
- **The rules of eye exist because the intuition is wrong in a known direction**: for two
  independent means, 95% intervals overlapping by about a quarter of one interval's length is
  p ≈ .05, and intervals that *just touch* is p ≈ .01 — not the .05 most readers assume.
- **You cannot introspect your own conventions.** Hinds found standard debiasing fails — being told
  about the bias, or imagining a novice, did not correct it; only **decomposing the task into
  concrete steps** helped. So list the reading steps and test one real reader from the segment on
  one question: "what does this say?" Never treat your own reading as evidence of readability.
- **Simplify the form, preserve the evidence.** The expert's real objection is "you have hidden what
  I need to check", not "this is too clear". Expert reactance to simplification is practitioner
  heuristic with no direct experimental support — carry it as one.

## Reader types — a prompt, not a classification
Decision-maker (chooses, then **forwards the image without you**) · analyst-peer (reproduces,
disputes — attacks binning, baselines, windows) · general public (remembers a gist; **does not
attack**, so silence is not comprehension) · regulator-auditor (checks conformance — attacks
omissions and definitions changed between periods). This four-way cut is **practitioner synthesis,
not a validated model**; no study supports this particular cut, and no validated framework exists for
tailoring one dataset across audiences. What is empirical is that reader groups rank the semantic
levels of a description differently and significantly (Lundgard & Satyanarayan), and that segments
differ in processing mode on identical material. Read the *next action* column as load-bearing:
name the verb — decide, reproduce, remember, verify — before choosing a form.

## Failure modes
- **Calibrating on colleagues** — the two groups do not merely differ in speed; they look at different parts of the picture.
- **The expertise licence** — an expert audience taken as permission for error bars, legends and unlabelled conventions.
- **Debiasing by imagination** — "I'll just think like a novice." Tested; it does not work.
- **Mistaking silence for comprehension** — the public's characteristic behaviour is absorption without challenge.
- **Reifying the taxonomy** — defending a design because it "serves the analyst persona".
- **Designing for a job title** — "it's for the board" says nothing about whether they will decide, monitor or file.
