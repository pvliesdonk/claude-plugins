# The Honesty Line

Every representation emphasises something; an artefact that emphasised nothing would be a data dump.
So the useful question is never "is this neutral?" but **"is what I am doing to this audience
adaptation or argument?"** Two tests answer it, and they catch different things.

## Test one — form or substance
**Tailoring the form is adaptation. Tailoring the comparison, baseline, window or denominator by
audience is persuasion.** Precisely: if adapting changes *which conclusion is available*, that is
persuasion; if it changes only *how easily the same conclusion is reached*, that is adaptation.

| Adaptation | Persuasion |
|---|---|
| Icon array for the public, distribution for an analyst | Absolute change for one audience, relative for another |
| Three years on the slide, ten in the appendix | Three years because ten is less flattering |
| Plain-language title | A title asserting more than the interval supports |
| Aggregating regions to fit the page, split available | Aggregating regions because one undercuts the claim |
| Uncertainty as a range here, a distribution there | Uncertainty in the technical version, absent from the board version |

The right-hand column is rarely cynical. It is usually someone making the artefact "clearer" one edit
at a time — which is why the test operates on the artefact, not the intention.

## Test two — checkability
**Does the pattern survive the reader checking it, or does it depend on not being checked?** An
emphasis that holds up when they pull the numbers is emphasis; one that evaporates — the impression
came from the axis range, the omitted segment, the flattering window — was doing work the data could
not. Corollary: **omission does not stop being omission because it was outsourced to the reader.** A
default view that excludes the inconvenient category, with a filter that would restore it, is an
omission plus plausible deniability.

## Apply it — where the leverage actually is
- **The title.** Highest leverage, least policed. Slant flips the perceived message, recall matches
  the title more often than the chart, and a majority still rate the chart impartial (Kong et al.).
  An impeccable encoding under an over-reaching headline passes every design review you run.
- **Emphasis technique is a demonstrated persuasion technology.** Declutter plus highlight plus
  connected annotation measurably improves what readers take away — by the same mechanism as a
  slanted headline. The obligation is not to use it less but to aim it only at findings you would
  defend under cross-examination.
- **Minimalism is itself a viewing code.** Spareness makes a chart *read* as credible (Hullman &
  Diakopoulos), so stripping a chart does not discharge disclosure; it can make an undisclosed choice
  more persuasive by dressing it in the vocabulary of neutrality.
- **Walk the four editorial layers and name one omission at each** — data, visual representation,
  annotation (textual, graphical *and social*), interactivity. Omission is the choice least likely to
  be indicated, and the annotation-only audit is the self-serving version.
- **Diffuse rhetoric reads as neutral** — "it doesn't feel slanted" is evidence of nothing.

## What is demonstrated to mislead
Keep this list separate from the list of things that annoy designers. **Truncated axes,
area-as-quantity and manipulated aspect ratio** inflated reported interpretations by 58.5–129.5%
(Pandey et al., n=330); **inverted axes** reverse the perceived direction and are the one absolute
prohibition. But truncation's bias is **visual magnification, not misreading**: Correll et al. found
explicit break cues and gradient bottoms produced **no significant debiasing**, bar and line did
**not** differ (p=0.50, killing that taught distinction), and 78% of participants who correctly
answered a truncation literacy item were biased anyway. So "always start at zero" fails, disclosure
theatre fails, and the honest/dishonest dichotomy fails. **Set the axis range from the effect size
that matters, and say in words what that size is.** Not demonstrated to deceive: pie charts, mild 3D
on a single series, decorative embellishment — policing those spends authority you need elsewhere.

## The honest null
Convention, not finding — label it as such. **Plot the interval, not the point**; a wide interval
spanning zero is a picture of what you know. **Make the null the assertion**: "Current data cannot
distinguish X from Y" is a valid headline. **Show what would have been detectable**, so absence of
evidence becomes something the reader can evaluate. Do not silently substitute a proxy.

## Failure modes
- **The audience-specific denominator** — per-capita for one reader, absolute for another, because each "lands better".
- **The disappearing caveat** — the most serious breach, and almost never deliberate.
- **Disclosure theatre** — a break mark that discharges the author and debiases nobody.
- **The literacy defence** — "my audience knows to check the axis." So did 78% of participants.
- **Spare as a costume** — elegance doing persuasive work that disclosure does not earn.
- **The manufactured takeaway** — a headline asserted because the template demanded a "so what".
