# Re-Cutting Across Audiences

The work exists, was made for one audience, and a different audience now needs it. This is the most
common request in professional practice and the one with the least written about it. The instinctive
move — take the artefact and adjust it — is usually wrong.

## Start with the honest position
**No validated framework exists** for tailoring one body of material into multiple audience-specific
representations. The literature supplies only adjacent findings: literacy-by-format interactions,
that different readers value different semantic content, and task-specific display optimality.
Everything below is reasoned synthesis — better than improvising, and a reader who asks "what is that
based on?" deserves that answer rather than an over-loaded citation.

## The five invariants
These do not change when the audience does. They are what makes two artefacts versions of one work
rather than two claims.

1. **The claim** — the sentence the reader can say afterwards. Write it for each variant and compare;
   if they are not effectively identical, you have made two findings, and at most one is supported.
2. **The data** — same rows, same period, same exclusions.
3. **The uncertainty** — it may be expressed differently, never present in one version and absent in another.
4. **The denominator** — per what.
5. **The direction and magnitude of the effect.**

**Everything else is free:** form, density, number of views, level of aggregation *shown* (not
computed), where the explanation lives, vocabulary, entry point, what goes first, what goes to an
appendix. The dividing line: **if adapting changes which conclusion is available, that is persuasion;
if it changes only how easily the same conclusion is reached, that is adaptation.**

## The move — derive or restart, decided explicitly
**Starting again from the material is often better than deriving a variant, and is a legitimate
recommendation, not an admission of failure.** Three reasons to distrust the derive instinct:

- **Anchoring and fixation.** Working from one candidate rather than several produces worse outcomes,
  less divergence and measurable fixation (Dow et al., on real behavioural data). A finished artefact
  of your own is the strongest anchor there is: every move becomes an edit, not a decision.
- **Its decisions are invisible to you.** The first artefact encodes a hundred choices, right for the
  first audience, surviving unexamined into the second. The expert cannot introspect which of their
  conventions are conventions, and warning does not fix it; only decomposing the task into steps
  helps — and returning to the material *is* that decomposition.
- **The honest answer is often a different artefact entirely.** A twenty-panel grid and one annotated
  sentence-with-number are not neighbours in design space; no edit sequence connects them.

**Derive** when the occasion is genuinely adjacent — same reader type, one step of dwell apart.
**Restart** when the reader's next action changes. When restarting, put the old artefact away — not
minimised, closed.

## The layered set
Where several audiences must be served from one body of work, build layers, not a compromise chart —
all sharing **one claim sentence**, written first, which is what prevents drift.

| Layer | For | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| **Gist** | Anyone, in seconds | The claim as a sentence, one number and its comparator |
| **Comparison** | A decision-maker | The one comparison the decision turns on, uncertainty as a range |
| **Distribution** | An analyst or peer | Spread, n, outliers, denominators |
| **Source** | An auditor, or anyone checking | Table, definitions, exclusions, provenance |

## Failure modes
- **The resize** — same figure, new dimensions; every occasion variable changed and nothing else did.
- **The additive re-cut** — original density plus a bolted-on headline, serving neither audience.
- **Drift in the claim** — three versions saying three subtly different things, each edited from the last.
- **The disappearing caveat** — uncertainty present in the technical version, absent from the executive one. The most common serious breach, and almost never deliberate.
- **The denominator swap** — a framing that "lands better" because it says something different.
- **Refusing to restart** — fifteen edits negotiating a complex artefact down, when a blank page and twenty minutes would have produced something better.
