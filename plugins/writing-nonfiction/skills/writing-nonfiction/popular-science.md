# Popular Science

Explaining something you understand deeply to someone who doesn't — and who has **no obligation to keep reading**. The craft is to make a technical idea not just *clear* but *wanted*. The master obstacle is the **curse of knowledge**: your inability to feel what it's like not to already know this. A capable writer usually reaches for a good analogy and concrete detail; the moves they skip are **opening a curiosity gap first**, **flagging where the analogy breaks**, and **staying honest about the simplification**.

## The recipe

1. **Beat the curse of knowledge.** Externalise the reader (a curious layperson, no field knowledge, wants to understand not to be impressed). Define or banish every term of art.
2. **Open a curiosity gap before you fill it.** People don't want answers to questions they don't have (Loewenstein's information-gap theory). Make the reader wonder *"wait, how does that work?"* before you explain. Give just enough to make the gap vivid — total ignorance produces no curiosity.
3. **Tell a story, not a syllabus.** A general reader follows a story long after they'd abandon a lecture. The smallest engine is the ABT: "we knew X *and* Y, *but* this didn't fit, *therefore* here's what we found." Let the science be the payoff, not the premise.
4. **Explain by analogy.** Map the unfamiliar onto the familiar so the reader's existing knowledge does the work. Anchor to something genuinely familiar to *this* reader — and **flag where the analogy breaks** ("this picture stops working once we get to ___").
5. **Climb down the ladder of abstraction.** Expert prose lives at the top ("immune response"); popular science wins by going **concrete** — a specific thing, a particular moment, a number you can picture. State the idea up the ladder, then come down to make it real; readers remember the bottom rungs.
6. **Make it stick, and stay honest.** SUCCES as a final pass — **S**imple (one idea), **U**nexpected (the gap), **C**oncrete, **C**redible, **E**motional, **S**tories. And flag what you simplify ("the real picture is messier, but for our purposes…"); don't let a vivid analogy harden into a falsehood, and don't quietly conflate two different mechanisms into one tidy story.

## Required moves

A strong draft usually has a vivid analogy and concrete detail, then skips these. Check each:

- [ ] **Curiosity gap opened *before* the explanation** — the reader feels the question first.
- [ ] **Jargon defined or gone** — no unexplained term of art.
- [ ] **An analogy anchored in the reader's world** — and **its limits flagged**, so they don't over-extend it into a misconception.
- [ ] **Concrete, picturable specifics** — not stranded at abstraction.
- [ ] **One core idea**, told as a small story.
- [ ] **Honest about the simplification** — flag what's been smoothed over; don't merge distinct mechanisms or claim more than is true for the sake of a clean line.

## Failure modes

- **The curse of knowledge, unaddressed** — assumed knowledge, skipped steps, jargon.
- **Answers without questions** — explaining something the reader was never made to wonder about.
- **A syllabus, not a story** — information-dense and lifeless.
- **An analogy left to break silently** — a vivid comparison that hardens into a misconception (e.g. conflating two different systems because one analogy covers both).
- **Stranded at the top of the ladder** — abstraction with no concrete rung.
- **Oversimplification into falsehood** — clarity bought with accuracy.
