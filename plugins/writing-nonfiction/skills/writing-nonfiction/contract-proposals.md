# Contract-Research Proposals

A proposition asking a **specific company** to fund or commission applied research. The reader is impatient, commercial, and skeptical — reading to decide whether this is worth money and risk, not to admire the science. Everything below follows from taking that reader seriously.

## Three readers, one document

- **Economic buyer** — "what do we get, what's it cost, what's the risk?" Answer in the first paragraph.
- **Technical sponsor** — "is this credible, and genuinely new vs. what we already do?" Satisfy in the body, *with evidence*.
- **Gatekeeper** — "is it complete, on-spec, easy to say yes to?" Give no reason to bounce it.

## The recipe

1. **Qualify first.** Does the company feel this problem *now*? Is there a budget and a buyer? Can you state why *you* specifically are the right partner? If not, no prose rescues the pitch.
2. **Heilmeier brief — jargon-free.** Before drafting, answer in plain language: the objective; what's new; **why they can't just do it in-house or buy it off the shelf**; who cares and what changes; the risks; the cost; the time; how success is judged. A question you can't answer cleanly is a hole the reader will find — fix it now.
3. **Front-loaded one-pager.** Open with SCQA: **S**ituation (a fact about their world they already accept) → **C**omplication (the change/pressure that makes this matter *now*) → **Q**uestion (what they should do) → **A**nswer (your proposition + headline payoff + headline cost). Then: hook + stakes → the gap (why current approaches fall short) → the proposition as **two or three independent workstreams** → the payoff. For a long proposal this one-pager becomes a stand-alone executive summary, written last, leading with the ask.
4. **Value in the buyer's terms.** The section research pitches most often botch — see Required moves. Sell the *outcome they pay for*, not the *work*.
5. **Credible plan, cost, risk.** Workstreams, milestones, deliverables, cost. Name the real risks and how you retire them. Define the **exams**: concrete mid-point and final go/no-go checkpoints at which the buyer can judge the work passing or failing — a mid-point go/no-go bounds their downside and is itself a selling point. Match the pricing model to scope certainty (fixed-price for defined parts, time-and-materials for exploratory ones, said openly); state scope exclusions and assumptions.
6. **Revise for skimming.** Get a full draft down, *then* tune: each section's first sentence carries its bottom line; plain words over field jargon; sentences short enough to parse on a phone. If any draft was AI-assisted, strip synthetic-sounding prose — it signals low effort and undercuts credibility.

## Required moves

A strong first draft usually **nails the first two** and **skips the rest**. Check every box:

- [ ] **Front-load the proposition and the "now."** (Usually done.)
- [ ] **Company is the hero; you are the guide.** Not the research team as protagonist; the money is for *their* outcome. (Usually done.)
- [ ] **Value framed as a change the company feels** — revenue protected, cost avoided, risk retired, capability gained, time-to-market cut — quantified where you honestly can. *Not* methods, rigor, or novelty for their own sake.
- [ ] **2–3 win themes, threaded.** Each = a discriminator (something that differs from the alternatives **and** that this company values) + the benefit + proof. ≤25 words each; repeated across sections so whichever one the reader lands on reinforces the same reasons to choose you.
- [ ] **Neutralize the alternatives.** Say plainly why an off-the-shelf tool, a generalist consultancy, or doing-nothing / muddling-through in-house won't get them there. The status quo is the biggest competitor — beat it explicitly.
- [ ] **Evidence credibility; don't assert it.** Replace "deep, hands-on expertise" with relevant past performance, proof points, and the named people on the relevant work.
- [ ] **Independent workstreams.** Frame the plan so a setback in one doesn't sink the engagement — not a single dependent chain (assess → then everything depends on it).
- [ ] **Bound the downside.** Make the mid-point go/no-go exam and the headline cost visible, so saying yes is low-risk.

## Failure modes

- **Selling the work, not the outcome** — pages of method before the buyer knows what they get.
- **No "now"** — a real problem with no reason to solve it this quarter; easy to defer.
- **Asserted, not evidenced, credibility** — "deep expertise" with no proof point.
- **No differentiation** — benefits any vendor could claim; the alternatives left un-neutralized.
- **Dependent workstreams** — workstream 2 collapses if workstream 1 stumbles; read as one fragile bet.
- **Risk-free theatre** — claiming no risks; the technical sponsor stops trusting the rest.
- **Buried bottom line / jargon as credential** — the proposition on page three; field terms used to sound expert.
