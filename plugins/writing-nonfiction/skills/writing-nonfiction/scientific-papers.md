# Scientific Papers

A journal article has a double job: report the work, **and get past peer review and get read**. Most papers are never read beyond title and abstract, and many never pass the reviewers — so the craft is in the genre's machinery as much as the science. A capable writer usually handles the prose and the Introduction well; the moves they skip are the **process** ones below.

## The recipe

1. **Choose the target journal *before* you write.** It sets your audience, register, and format. Criteria in order: scope/fit (a mismatch is a desk-reject) → audience (specialist vs broad, which changes how you write the Introduction and Discussion) → impact → indexing/cost. Start from the journals you cite.
2. **Find the story.** A paper is an argument with one take-home message, not a lab notebook. OCAR: Opening (context) → Challenge (the question) → Action (what you did/found) → Resolution (what it means). If you can't state the finding as one sentence, you don't have the paper yet.
3. **Build on IMRaD; write each section to its job.**
   - **Introduction** — CARS: establish the territory → the **niche/gap** (most-skipped) → occupy it with your aim. Funnel broad to specific.
   - **Methods** — enough to replicate; past tense; no interpretation.
   - **Results** — findings, neutrally; figures/tables carry the data; no "what it means."
   - **Discussion** — interpret, re-broaden to significance, state limitations honestly, land the take-home.
4. **Write the screening pair last: title + abstract.** They travel separately and decide whether anyone reads the paper. Title: specific, conveys the finding; avoid "Role of…/Effect of…/Towards…" (caveat: broad-audience journals prefer noun-phrase titles — check the style guide). Abstract: informative/structured, front-load the finding, stands alone. Plus keywords for discoverability.
5. **Figures, references, authorship.** Each figure self-contained and decluttered, leading with the point it makes. Cite in the journal's style; verify every citation supports the claim. Settle authorship by the **ICMJE criteria** (all four); record contributions (CRediT).
6. **Cover letter, submit, survive review.** A <1-page cover letter to the editor (contribution + fit *in your own words*, not the abstract; original, not under simultaneous review). Then desk-check → 2–3 reviewers → decision. On revision, the **response-to-reviewers letter** matters as much as the revision.

## Required moves

A strong draft nails the Introduction/CARS and the prose, then skips the process. Check each:

- [ ] **Journal chosen first**, with the writing pitched to its audience and format.
- [ ] **One take-home message**, stated as a sentence and built toward (not a findings dump).
- [ ] **The Introduction names the gap** (CARS Move 2), not just a field summary.
- [ ] **Section jobs kept clean** — no interpretation in Results, no methods in Discussion, limitations stated honestly.
- [ ] **Title + abstract written to earn the read** — specific title, stand-alone informative abstract, finding front-loaded.
- [ ] **Authorship by ICMJE; citations verified** — every reference exists and supports its claim.
- [ ] **Response-to-reviewers discipline** (on revision): address **every** point — quote it, give your response, state the change and where; courteous even in disagreement; one or two polite refusals per round at most.

## Failure modes

- **No target journal until the end** — writing genre-blind, then reformatting and re-pitching.
- **A lab-notebook paper** — everything reported, no story or take-home.
- **No gap in the Introduction** — a field summary that never justifies the work.
- **A weak title/abstract** — the screening pair that doesn't earn a read.
- **Blurred section jobs** — interpretation in Results, methods in Discussion.
- **A thin or combative response-to-reviewers letter** — skipping points or arguing defensively; a rejection risk independent of the science.
