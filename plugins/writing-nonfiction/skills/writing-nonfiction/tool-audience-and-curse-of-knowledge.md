# Audience & the Curse of Knowledge

Who you write for, and the cognitive reason experts write unclearly.

## Audience analysis
Name the actual reader(s) — not "the audience". Many documents have three:
- **Primary** — the person who acts on it (the decision-maker). Write the bottom line for them, first.
- **Secondary** — the expert who scrutinises detail (the technical reviewer). Satisfy them in the body, with evidence.
- **Gatekeeper** — who must pass it on (procurement, an editor). Give them no reason to bounce it.

Pitch depth, jargon, and structure to *these specific people* — what they know, what they need, what they'll do with it.

## The curse of knowledge (Pinker)
Once you know something well, you can't imagine not knowing it — so you skip steps, assume jargon, and abbreviate concepts the reader has never met. It is **the single best explanation of why good people write bad prose**. It isn't arrogance; it's a genuine inability to simulate the reader's ignorance.

**The remedy — externalise the reader:**
- Show a draft to a real member of the audience; their confusion marks the steps your knowledge let you skip.
- Define or cut every term of art.
- Mind the *gap*, not just hard words — the curse hides in missing connective steps as much as in jargon.
- Adopt "classic style": prose as a window onto the world — point at the thing, plainly, as if showing a competent friend.

## Failure modes
- **Writing for yourself** — a display of knowledge instead of service to the reader.
- **One-size prose** — ignoring that primary / secondary / gatekeeper read differently.
- **Skipped steps** — connective reasoning obvious only to you.
