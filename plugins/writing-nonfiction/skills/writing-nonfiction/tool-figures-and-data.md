# Figures & Data

When a figure, table, or data point must do persuasive or evidential work.

## Principles (honest, accurate graphics)
- **Maximise the data-ink ratio (Tufte):** remove everything that isn't carrying meaning — gridlines, borders, 3-D, chartjunk.
- **Match the encoding to the reader's task (Cleveland & McGill):** people read *position* (bars, dots on a common scale) accurately and *angle/area* (pie charts) poorly. Prefer position.
- **Self-contained:** a figure plus its caption should be understandable without the surrounding text — numbered, labelled, sourced.

## Narrative (the figure makes a point)
- **Lead with the point, not the chart (Knaflic).** State what the data *means* — an "assertion headline" ("Returns fell 31% after the redesign") — and use the figure as proof. Don't present a chart and let the reader infer the conclusion.
- **Direct the eye** with one deliberate cue (colour / size / position) so the key point lands *preattentively* — before the reader consciously reads the chart. Everything else recedes.
- **Declutter** so the signal is the only thing left.

## Apply it
- For each figure ask: what one thing must the reader believe after seeing it? Design the figure to make *that* point, and write the caption/headline to state it.
- Strip non-data ink; check the encoding is position-based where accuracy matters.

## Failure modes
- **Data dump** — every chart shown, the conclusion left to the reader.
- **Topic headline** — "Sales by region" instead of "The North drove all growth".
- **Misleading encoding** — pies / 3-D / truncated axes that distort.
