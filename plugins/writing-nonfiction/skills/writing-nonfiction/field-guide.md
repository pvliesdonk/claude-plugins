# Hands-On Field Guides & Handbooks

A practitioner how-to document — a do-this-now handbook or field guide — for a **qualified-but-non-specialist** reader who needs to *act*, will skim, may jump in anywhere, and stops reading the moment the prose stops helping them act. (Worked example: a product-security handbook for SMEs.) A capable writer usually leads with the problem and a task; the moves they skip are **staged maturity** and a **clean how-to/why separation**.

## The recipe

1. **Name the genre blend and the reader.** "Handbook / field guide / white paper / guide" set different expectations; a practitioner doc usually blends them — make the blend explicit ("a practical guide you can act on, with the reasoning behind each step"). The reader is competent in their job but not in your field, short on time, lands on any page first.
2. **Lead with tasks (minimalism).** Get the reader onto a real, meaningful task immediately; minimise passive reading; make sections self-contained (each is an entry point). A handbook that opens with three pages of background has already lost them.
3. **Separate the *do* from the *why* (Diátaxis).** A hands-on guide is **how-to-dominant with supporting explanation**, and the two must not blur. Keep the steps clean; put the rationale in a clearly-marked aside ("why this matters"), so the reader who just wants to act isn't wading through theory and the reader who wants to understand can find it.
4. **Structure: problem → do-this-now → staged maturity.** Front-load the problem and payoff (a lead/exec-summary for the skimmer) → the immediate concrete actions in priority order → then sequence further advice as a **now → next → later** maturity progression ("do these baseline things now; once they hold, do the next tier"), not a flat list → a clear next step at the end.
5. **Make it usable.** Runnable **checklists** (pair each item with a one-line "what *and* why" — your reader isn't an expert); concrete worked examples; good/better/best (tiered) recommendations; plain language; a figure or table (a decision flow, a tier table) where it beats prose.

## Required moves

A strong draft usually leads with the problem and a first task, then skips these. Check each:

- [ ] **Reader and genre blend named** — a specific non-specialist, expectations set.
- [ ] **Action before theory** — the reader can *do* something within the first page or two.
- [ ] **How-to and why visibly separated** — clean steps; rationale in marked asides, not tangled into the instructions.
- [ ] **Staged by maturity (now → next → later)** — a path, not a flat overwhelming list; a small team isn't asked to boil the ocean.
- [ ] **Checklists that teach** — each item paired with a plain-language "what/why."
- [ ] **Plain language, self-contained sections, a clear next step.**

## Failure modes

- **Background before action** — pages of theory before anything the reader can do.
- **Blurring how-to and explanation** — steps tangled with rationale; neither reader served.
- **A flat, overwhelming list** — no prioritisation or maturity staging.
- **Writing for the expert** — jargon and assumed knowledge; the curse of knowledge with the wrong audience.
- **Checklists without the why** — expert-style checklists handed to a non-expert with no teaching.
- **Genre confusion** — handbook expectations, white-paper delivery (or vice versa).
